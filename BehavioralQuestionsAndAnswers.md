## BUGS

<summary>Example questions & their background</summary>

>
> - What was the hardest bug you encountered? What did you learn from it?

- **Paige**: *Situation*: Broken model training, affecting multiple teams: depending on a config switch, either performance was hit (eventually deadlocking) or a memory leak occurred (eventually OOM) far into training (hours/days), and distributed training and limited logging further complicated matters; *Task*: My model training task was affected, so rather than waiting for anyone to pick up the troubleshooting, I picked it up myself. *Action*: By successive simplification (dataset reduction, stubbing parts of pipeline) I could reproduce the training pipeline failure much sooner, and then by process of elimination of possible culprits (and adding much needed logging and correlation IDs along the path of my investigation) I narrowed down the epicenter to the tiling part of the pipeline, then discovered the deadlock was down to repeated access to the same file on a distributed file system (each DDP replica was running tiling) and then a recent caching change to tiling was the cause of the memory leak; so I separated tiling from data loading and refactored the caching change; *Result*: teams could continue, I learned that part of the pipeline which helped me down the line, and added one more reason to phase out Prefect (to lift restrictions on pipeline organization). This also helped me later on in tracking down and resolving memory leaks in other products and parts of the pipeline, e.g. CNN aggregator and FCN segmentation models.
- **Paige**: Hit a numerical instability issue when exploring the possibility of standardizing the inference cluster on A10 (and phase out use of T4), cordoned off the affected models to still run on T4, resolved eventually after the model was retrained with AMP (Automatic Mixed Precision).
- **Paige**: Discovered and fixed a long-standing cross-cutting bug (predating my joining) that silently skipped edge tiles during both training and inference, present in shared code used across many teams and modules.
- **Helsing**: *Situation*: Vision processing pipeline operating at 10 FPS, despite the compute not being fully utilized; *Action*: Profiling to identify biggest culprits, sorted & prioritized primarily by compute budget impact and secondarily by ease of rework - then applied architectural changes across the integrating points to sensor/image acquisition and actuation: concurrency, vectorization of hotspots, pipelining of processing phases (sync on pipeline phases, not the full pipeline execution); *Result*: almost tripled the pipeline throughput (FPS), unblocked use of a heavier ML model, filled the roadmap with further planned improvements (for a more powerful platform).
- **Philips Lifeline**: *Situation*: While extending the PERS device for Lifeline, I encountered random errors on UART between CPU & 3G modem; *Action*: eventually I collected enough observations to notice that certain bit patterns are more prone to error, I placed a longer repeated such sequence of commands at boot time which allowed me to replicate it reliably, asked a colleague to teach me how to use an oscilloscope, compared the sequence sent by the processor with what was logged by the modem, confirming a discrepancy, went back to compare modem & quartz clock specs and concluded that the tolerance of the autobauding mechanism was close to the precision boundaries of the clock (though still within the range), turned off autobauding and locked the UART frequency; *Result*: the issue was fixed, I am more cautious before I trust a hardware specification, it also taught me the value of teaming up cross-functionally and going beyond one's own field to solve bigger problems.
- **Philips**: *Situation*: Working on an H.264 decoder and optimizing it for a multicore DSP processor (the IC still under development at the time), I was experiencing a deadlock upon adding profiling instrumentation, and there was no debugger available on top of SystemC emulation; *Action*: Running the code on a different processor architecture (but the same OS) pointed to a kernel-related issue with the port of eCos, comparing the ports pointed to a system-wide mutex inserted into a system call; *Result*: selective disabling of the mutex confirmed root cause, instrumenting directly on SystemC dodged the issue altogether and bought time for the team porting the OS to correct the use of the mutex - it taught me to think outside the box, seek advice directly from the team involved with the problematic feature, check my implicit assumptions, and I became familiar with tools for working with large code bases.
- **Siemens**: *Situation*: 3G network billing, serialization heisenbug which occurred only in production (with no access to that environment); *Action*: selective instrumentation to limit performance degradation allowed to track down the crash to Java's own serialization implementation, reimplemented a subset of serialization needed for the service and introduced resource preallocation to take Java's garbage collection out of the critical path; *Result*: Eliminated the crash. Realized that even mature third-party code can have bugs which only occur under system stress, the choice to reimplement serialization without reflection also raised performance 2x (and later compounded with DB mocks to 10x improvement for the testing harness).

**Paige**:

- **Model packaging and release, from multi-sprint ritual to a one-day operation**:
  - **Context**: **Paige products are AI helping pathologists find and grade cancer** (classification, regression and segmentation) **in microscope imagery of suspected cancer tissue** extracted from the body.
  - **Problem**: **Each product is one (or more) trained model(s), released as a regulated medical service. Releasing a trained clinical AI/ML model was a manual, two-sprint, team-wide process**. Every product had bespoke inference configuration, custom output transformation, hand-written packaging boilerplate code and manual regulatory acceptance test runs, and improvements and fixes to the wrapping code for one product were not carried over to others. **The company wanted to expand** from a handful of products (3 for prostate, 2 for breast) **to a broad portfolio** (prostate, breast, colon, and other tissue, as well as biomarkers such as HER2, MSI). **At that release cost, the portfolio simply could not scale**: the team throughput was the bottleneck.
  - **Ownership**: **Nobody asked me to resolve the situation**, but I saw the same pain repeated across teams. I scoped it myself to two products at first, and drove it from design through rollout as the engineer implementing it. Later I also carried its ownership and further development as team leader for the clinical products, so I also had to defend the capacity for it against other requests and responsibilities.
  - **Technical Approach**: What did I ship:
    - **Shared Python package capable of running any of the trained AI/ML models** allowing common config values to be passed (dimensions of input and other tiling parameters, model quantization, operating points for classification), **and transforming the model inference output object into API objects** (classes, regression values, heatmaps, list of points of interest)
    - **Compact and executable config** representation (hydra-core) **and standardized** config **template** (cookiecutter)
    - **Automated script which, starting from a training URL**, extracted the necessary configuration values, downloaded the trained model and **created a Python package for the product** with the help of the wrapper and the configuration template. **Products became a thin bit of configuration plus a shared tooling library rather than a new codebase each time**.
    - The **central trade-off was freedom versus standardization**. I deliberately did **not standardize the experimentation/training phase**, only the path from a trained artifact to a released one. That decision proved to be crucial for its subsequent broad acceptance.
    - A **second trade-off was to keep the templating compact and human-readable**, because it had to be accessible to a wider audience - **data scientists, junior engineers and even regulatory staff** for audit purposes.
    - I faced some technical challenges along the way, such as: I needed to decouple the processing DAG, splitting tiling/embedding from detection and classification (which made the pieces simpler to configure, but also easier to compose and test), and I needed to engineer around the Docker image size limits for a foundation model (going to FP16 weights turned out to be an acceptable compromise for a release deadline).
  - **Production Delivery**: All of it was under FDA, CE-IVDR and ISO constraints: versioned artifacts, traceable provenance, isolated training and verification datasets, regulatory acceptance tests.
  - **Adoption**: I made six products compatible myself, then taught teammates to port the rest rather than doing it for them, but I paid close attention to their feedback during adoption and shipped improvements on my end.
  - **Outcome**: Release went from a two-sprint, team-wide effort to a couple-of-days, single-engineer operation. Faster time to market, error-prone manual steps eliminated, and the teams could scale to a growing product portfolio.
- **Automated regulatory acceptance tests**:
  - **Situation**: I observed repeated struggle across multiple teams with a fragmented, manual, error-prone regulatory acceptance test process - knowledge was scattered, no one was tasked with the upkeep and no one asked me to resolve this either.
  - **Action**: I took it on unprompted, **collapsed a fragmented multi-step workflow** (AWS credential setup, remote/local inference, compute allocation, test execution) **into a single script**, added performance improvements into the shared testing library (**parallelizing tests**, and **decoupling inference execution from test execution through caching, radically cutting test runtime** for many products), also consolidated two competing partial implementations, made six products compatible, guided colleagues for the rest. What is important to mention is that I rallied the operations team to **bring execution of acceptance tests under CI as weekly runs**, and as the default path rather than an opt-in.
  - **Result**: Faster releases across all teams (**config/templating work reduced it to a couple of days, this took release to one day**), lasting reduction in the risk of issues going undetected until release time (it even **reduced the rate of high-prio defects** - from once a month to once every couple months).
- **Disagreement on standardization**:
  - **Situation**: When I assumed the team lead role, I was already pushing for standardization around the model packaging (and around PyTorch Lightning) to scale teams, however, one data scientist strongly opposed it, arguing it would restrict his freedom to experiment. He was a very strong contributor: always shipped on time and to high quality, but worked detached from the team for long stretches. If I had forced it through as team lead, I would have won the decision and lost the person.
  - **Action**: I judged that regular 1:1 chats would not work. Instead I decided to reserve time to pair with him at the natural touch points in his workflow and learn how he actually worked; along the way I extended the framework standardizing inference to cover gaps we identified together and shared this back with him for first feedback.
  - **Result**: He learned the benefits of the framework: he could release faster, stopped copying boilerplate, reclaimed his focus time, and became an advocate himself; then I could offload the pairing to a more junior team member without much change to his WoW. My curiosity about his scepticism paid off for both me and him.
- **Pushing back on the foundation model as an embedder**:
  - **Situation**: While adjacent to the foundation model team, there was a push to adopt the FM as the embedder for prostate diagnostics.
  - **Action**: As clinical team lead I pushed back, citing regulatory constraints on regressing already reported metrics, the compute cost implications at FM scale, and a security fix that was already on a hard deadline.
  - **Result**: The decision was deferred rather than forced through on enthusiasm. Separately, on the infrastructure side, I resolved the Docker image size blocker for the FM weights, so the release of a dedicated FM-based product still landed on time.
- **Bridge**: This is the shape of work companies are hiring for: often an existing, non-greenfield codebase, several teams with their own habits, and a change that only counts if other teams adopt it. The engineering is the easy half.
- **Ownership beyond the immediate team**:
  - Driving development of ML tooling and infrastructure to improve the scale and efficiency of ML development.
  - Contributed to company-wide effort to break down the monolithic internal ML libraries.
  - Maintained platform-wide security and dependency hygiene: updated Python and base container image versions across all modules, and kept library dependencies current and consistent across multiple teams' codebases.
  - Tooling to shorten the iteration of the eval-to-production loop for selection of the model operating point (threshold) by clinical pathologists (heatmaps/tissuemaps masking); knowledge sharing around the available UI modalities back to the team
  - In a hackathon, I built an image registration pipeline to prepare data for stain-invariant tuning of clinical cancer diagnostics models.

**Helsing**:

- **Problem**: The team iterated rapidly on the computer vision pipeline, but there was no shared, regular measurement of whether the changes helped. Metrics came out of ad-hoc scripts and manual one-off runs, with no baseline and no history, so an improvement or a regression typically surfaced late, at integration or in field testing.
- **My ownership**: I acted on this as an opportunity to build a regression monitoring harness alongside my optimization work.
- **Technical approach**: An ETL pipeline that processes field-collected telemetry and imagery and extracts algorithm performance metrics from pipeline runs, evaluation runs nightly in GitLab CI, with results tracked against a past baseline with trend tracking (visualized with Plotly, served from Neptune) that the scientific staff read themselves. The deliberate trade-off: a rolling-window dataset rather than a full one, so numbers are comparable across changes but without getting stuck on a set with outdated data modalities (though on a slower cadence we also evaluated on full academic datasets).
- **Delivery and adoption**: The team universally adopted it and every metric movement became attributable to a change in code. It turned the team's iterations from a subjective into a data-driven feedback loop. The team gained confidence and moved to releasing on a rolling basis to field tests and production.
- **Outcome**: Regressions got caught early instead of at integration, the team could see improvement as a trend rather than speculate, and my own 2.8x pipeline throughput optimization work was only safe to do at that pace because each step was measured against the baseline.
- **Bridge**: That approach is what I would bring to any AI-first rollout. If you cannot see the before and after, you are running on enthusiasm.

**Paige: Model-assisted data labeling that fed improved production models (2D segmentation for tissue segmentation; categorical labeling for the foundation model)**

- **Problem**: Training data for pathology models was limited by labeling. A large portion of slides had *no segmentation labels* (alt.: only unstructured, free-form pathology reports, and no structured cancer/benign, severity or tissue-class label). Additionally slides get rescanned with different staining, producing near-duplicates that quietly pollute training and evaluation splits. (Alt.: our prior solutions based on RegExp or keyword matching were of limited use).
- **My ownership**: The idea of using a model-assisted labeling (alt. LLM) came from a conversation with a scientist, and I jointly designed and built the workflow (alt.: while adjacent to the foundation model team).
- **Approach**: This involved building *an iterative, manual, and partial 2D labeling loop with rapid training of a smaller model generalizing segmentation on manual 2D labels* (alt.: LLM summarization and extraction over the unstructured reports to assign coarse labels and flag duplicates. Using the unstructured reports which DID have labels to iterate on the prompt. Further refined the labeling workflow by relying on the consensus between the LLM-extracted label, output of a tissue-specific model where we had one already, and the RegExp-based tool, so multiple independent signals had to agree).
- **Limitations and guardrails**: The 2D (alt. LLM) labels were then treated as weak supervision, not ground truth. Anything used to report metrics stayed fully human-labeled. Disagreement between the labeling (*segmentation*; alt.: LLM) model, *the prior computer vision algorithm* (alt.: a narrow specialty model and RegExp) was a signal to escalate and review.
- **Outcome**: Substantially more of the archive became usable as training data, which is what let the scientists scale to *more accurate* (alt. larger) models. (alt. Virchow foundation model for computational pathology is now public on Hugging Face and the pan-cancer detection product runs on it in production - where the prior models we trained were around 20M parameters, the foundation model was 630M).

**Helsing: AI to ramp up fast, on unfamiliar stack**

- **Problem**: I joined a team working on a computer vision and telemetry pipeline running at 10 FPS with the compute nowhere near saturated, and I had to be productive on it quickly. The performance work could not wait for me to be comfortable.
- **My ownership**: The optimization was mine end to end, and so was the decision to use AI to compress the ramp-up rather than reading my way in.
- **How AI actually entered the workflow**:
  - **Code comprehension first, not code generation**: I had an agent walk me through subsystems (extraction of architectural diagrams and descriptions) and explain why the code was shaped that way, then verified the extracted claims against the code empirically. That is where the biggest time saving was.
  - **Bridge documentation gaps**: For nix, published docs are stale, but it produces robust error output, so I used it to ground the work, letting the build and the error messages be the arbiter. In short: I proposed the designs, the agent iterated on implementation, the nix system decides - sufficient for scaffolding code, not for the core algorithmic part.
  - **Then my own tooling**: As my code review load grew, I built custom agentic tools (first Anthropic SDK, later LangChain) with MCP over past code reviews applied to refinement of agent skills guarding me from my blind spots and collecting items for my learning process (and similar for troubleshooting).
- **Guardrails**: Actual profiling and optimization work was fully my own (as opposed to the nix scaffolding). All of it was subject to the normal code reviews, CI runs (incl. linters, type checks, dependency policy, etc.). And in my last months I was expanding agentic use while taking the optimizations to the next level on top of CUDA.
- **Delivery and outcome:** I ramped up fast on algebra-heavy code, profiled the code in-situ, ranked culprits by compute budget impact first and ease of rework second, then made architectural changes across the integration points to data acquisition and actuation. I applied: concurrency, pipelining, vectorization of the hotspots, and TensorRT with engine caching. It goes back to my time at Philips where I was part of the research department. Modeled after Bell Labs, it operated in two modes. Either a product department came to it with a difficult problem they could not solve themselves, or the research department initiated R&D on a new product or service, which also typically meant facing a challenge nobody had solved before. Projects took on average no longer than a year and frequently you had more than one going. And I spent 13 years there. My mode of operation there was to get familiar with a new domain very quickly and the projects ranged across entire Philips's portfolio, including semiconductors, consumer electronics, fitness, and healthcare (so I could not rely on my learning compounding). I also needed to get the lay of the land very quickly, which meant finding out who the important stakeholders and decision makers were, connecting with the product team members, scanning the most recent research in the area to understand the solution space, as well as the intellectual property landscape. For some of the projects, it was also important to team up with partners through European projects to combine the necessary capabilities, or with standardization bodies to ensure interoperability. That's the tool set, but what were the example challenges?

For example, for the personal emergency response system, I needed to find a way for the device to localize itself with minimal battery consumption, and off-the-shelf GPS modules exceeded the energy budget. Then I could find a cut-off point at which the computation becomes too expensive, but the data is already reduced sufficiently to be transmitted with small enough energy expense. Next, I needed to find components for that part of the GPS module and have the product team members integrate it with the system. Then I implemented the necessary computation and transmission myself. Along the way, I needed to implement it in such a way as to avoid the necessity for licensing of patents owned by some of the GPS manufacturers and write an invention disclosure for the alternative embodiment I found, to submit for a patent.

Then rinse and repeat - new domain, new team, new challenge, innovative solution, brought to production.

## TECH: SCALE

<details>
<summary>Example questions & their background</summary>

> ❓
>
> - What's the largest scale you've experienced in your career?

</details>

Scale showed up in different shapes across my career - event throughput, sensor telemetry, and training data - so let me give a few examples:

- **Paige (training data):** Training datasets ranged from tens to hundreds of thousands of whole-slide pathology images, each multiple GB and yielding tens of thousands of tissue tiles - so up to billions of tiles and hundreds of terabytes up to petabyte scale. On a cluster of DGX nodes with V100 or A100 GPUs, embedding tiles with a ResNet and training the CNN aggregator took a day for 10k up to a week for 100k slides (depending on resource availability on the shared cluster), with tile decoding and I/O rather than the GPUs usually being the bottleneck. Pretraining the pathology foundation model itself ran on over a million slides, and even just generating Virchow embeddings for own 100k-slide experiments took weeks.
- **OSRAM (multi-tenant IIoT):** The platform grew to ~30 tenants with hundreds of sensors each, every sensor reporting multiple data points a minute - almost a hundred billion data points a year - which drove the move from plain PostgreSQL to TimescaleDB (hypertables, compression) and later to a managed instance. The DLI decision-support tools were additionally backed by Copernicus global solar radiation data - ~50GB per year at hourly resolution - serving derived DLI for sales reps and growers.
- **Philips (telemetry volume):** I built the fall prediction/prevention service with capacity to ingest telemetry from ~130k subscribers' wearables - a 2 Hz pressure sensor and a 25 Hz 3-axis accelerometer each - which is about 10 million samples per second in aggregate, roughly 1.7 TB/day raw - and with my custom compression that still came to about 0.5 TB/day, some 150-200 TB per year. Devices uploaded opportunistically over WiFi (falling back to 3G), and a monthly batch on AWS decompressed the data, ran filtering and DSP, and scored it with an SVM compiled to native code - all within roughly $2 per customer per month, predating modern managed MLOps tooling.
- **Siemens (event throughput):** I built real-time metering and billing for per-second charging, as part of the Siemens 3G network control platform, which went into production at operators such as Vodafone, Vodacom and China Unicom. Per-second charging of prepaid calls means reserving and re-granting quota in real time during each call, so an operator the size of Vodafone (~130M subscribers in 2003) generates on the order of hundreds of billions of charging events a year - tens of thousands per second at peak - where every event translates to money, and latency directly affects the call.

The common lesson: at scale the design choice matters more than the hardware - e.g. quota reservations, autoscaling, rightsizing, custom compression, opportunistic uploads, caching of embeddings - this decides whether the system is affordable at all.
